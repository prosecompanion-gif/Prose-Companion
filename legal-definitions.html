Word of the Day

Each week, new legal terms are added alongside each episode. Learn the language of the courtroom one word at a time.

Note: These definitions are simplified for general understanding. Legal terms can have specific meanings that vary by jurisdiction. Always check your state's rules and statutes for exact definitions.

Episode 1

Pro Se (also "Pro Per")

A Latin term meaning "for oneself." A pro se litigant is a person who represents themselves in court without an attorney.

Plaintiff

The person or party who starts a lawsuit by filing a complaint with the court. In criminal cases, this is usually the government (the prosecution).

Defendant

The person or party being sued (in a civil case) or charged with a crime (in a criminal case).

Complaint / Petition

The first document filed to start a lawsuit. It explains what happened, who is involved, and what the plaintiff wants the court to do.

Answer

The defendant's written response to the complaint. It addresses each claim and states any defenses. There is usually a strict deadline to file this.

Motion

A formal written request asking the court to make a decision or take an action. Examples: Motion to Dismiss, Motion for Summary Judgment, Motion to Compel.

Filing

Submitting a document to the court so it becomes part of the official case record. Many courts now require electronic filing (e-filing).

Service / Service of Process

The legal requirement to deliver court documents to the other party. There are specific rules about how documents must be delivered (served).

Docket

The official record or log of all filings, hearings, and actions in a case. When you "access your case files," you are typically looking at the docket.

Discovery

The phase of a lawsuit where both sides exchange information and evidence. This includes interrogatories (written questions), depositions (recorded testimony), and document requests.

Hearing

A proceeding before a judge where arguments are heard. Not all hearings involve witnesses — many are just oral arguments on motions.

Order

A written decision by a judge directing something to happen or ruling on a motion. Court orders are legally binding.

Judgment

The final decision of the court that resolves the case. It determines who wins, and may include money damages or other relief.

Appeal

A request to a higher court to review and change the decision of a lower court. There are strict deadlines and procedures for filing an appeal.

Statute of Limitations

The deadline for filing a lawsuit. After this time passes, you generally lose the right to bring that claim. The time limit varies by type of case and by state.

Jurisdiction

The authority of a court to hear a case. A court must have jurisdiction over the subject matter and the parties involved.

Venue

The specific geographic location (county or district) where a case should be filed. Usually, this is where the events occurred or where the defendant lives.

Continuance

A postponement of a court hearing or trial to a later date. You typically must file a motion requesting a continuance and show good cause.

Subpoena

A court order requiring a person to appear in court to testify, or to produce documents. Ignoring a subpoena can result in contempt of court.

Contempt of Court

Disobeying or disrespecting a court order or the court itself. Can result in fines or jail time.

Brief

A written document submitted to the court that argues your legal position. It cites relevant laws, rules, and case law to support your argument.

Case Law / Precedent

Past court decisions that establish legal principles. Courts generally follow precedent from higher courts in the same jurisdiction.

Affidavit

A written statement of facts made under oath. It is signed before a notary public and can be used as evidence in court.

Default Judgment

A judgment entered against a party who fails to respond to a complaint or appear in court within the required time. If you are sued, you MUST respond by the deadline or risk losing automatically.

Summary Judgment

A decision made by the court without a full trial, when the facts are undisputed and one party is clearly entitled to win as a matter of law.

New words are added each week with every episode. If you encounter a legal term you do not understand, check your state court's self-help website or Cornell Law Institute's legal dictionary at law.cornell.edu/wex.
